Extending the computation to k = 75: 16 new terms, each verified minimal.

Taking rickyc's two results above as a *search accelerator* (the unique failing
index satisfies e = n mod k, and for each p^a || k,
p^(a + v_p(C(k-1,e)) + floor(log_p max(e, k-1-e))) divides n - e), every solution
with failing index r lies on the single progression n = r mod D(k,r), where D(k,r)
is the product of those prime powers. Sweeping all k such progressions below a
candidate is therefore exhaustive rather than heuristic. That extends OEIS
A389360, whose b-file ends at k = 59, by 16 terms:

   k          n_k                 k          n_k
  60   2117441088029             68     260686545186
  61        569181900            69     709479687556
  62     155275274056            70   66383308992066
  63     216949362182            71      13760436550
  64       2600001558            72     890473430071
  65   1936512990784             73      40861892736
  66   1618958347825             74    3146202243081
  67       1178168706            75   32639932074432

Each is minimal, not merely a solution: the sweeps below them tested
89,015,468,839 candidates in total with zero hits. As an arithmetic check on
coverage, the candidate count predicted independently from sum_r floor(n_k/D(k,r))
is 89,015,469,493, agreeing with the number actually enumerated to 7 parts in 10^9
(the difference is the n >= 2k floor). Positive control: the identical code path
re-derives all 58 published terms k = 2..59 with zero mismatches -- it found two
real bugs in the process (taking the first hit in r-order rather than the minimum
over r, and omitting the n >= 2k floor). Separately, a verifier sharing no code
with the searcher builds C(n,k) as an exact integer (451 to 905 decimal digits)
and tests divisibility straight from the definition: for all 16 terms there is
exactly one failing divisor, always at index n mod k.

On StijnC's question of what estimate one should expect here, the extra terms say
the following.

(1) No new extreme in 38 further terms. n_k^(1/k) stays inside the band observed
for k <= 37: over 2 <= k <= 75 the maximum is still k = 10 (2.1781) and the
minimum still k = 31 (1.3368). The new terms only span 1.3658 (k = 67) to 1.6048
(k = 60).

(2) The prime/composite separation sharpens rather than washing out. Mean of
log(n_k)/k:

     k range    primes   composites
      2-19      0.4954     0.5825
     20-39      0.3390     0.5122
     40-59      0.3521     0.4502
     60-75      0.3264     0.4105

Regressing log(n_k)/k on k over k >= 20 gives slope -0.0026 for composite k but
-0.0002 for prime k. The apparent decay lives almost entirely on the composite
branch; the prime branch is close to flat near 0.33 across 55 terms. If the truth
is c^((1+o(1))k), these data are consistent with two different constants for the
two branches rather than one.

(3) The lower bound is still very far from the truth in this range:
n_k / LB(k) runs from 1.3e6 (k = 64) to 6.4e8 (k = 75), i.e. log(LB(k))/k is
roughly 0.07 to 0.23 against log(n_k)/k of roughly 0.31 to 0.47.

This does not resolve #1063 and claims no new theorem. The structural results are
rickyc's; they are used here but not re-proved formally, so minimality is
conditional on them (it is verified empirically on all 58 published terms as well
as the 16 new ones -- a Lean formalisation of the divisibility result would close
that gap). Machine arithmetic is u64; all values are below 2^63 and D(k,r)
overflow is detected rather than silently wrapped.
